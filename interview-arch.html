AWS·01 Walk me through how you'd design a multi-tier VPC for a production workload. Foundation

Why they ask: Tests whether you understand subnet segmentation, routing, and defense-in-depth.

Masterstroke answer

I start with CIDR planning and leave room to grow — a /16 for the VPC, /24s for subnets, and I document the allocation plan so future VPCs don't overlap when we peer or attach to a Transit Gateway. Three tiers across three AZs: public subnets hold only the ALB and NAT Gateways, private app subnets run EKS or ECS, and private data subnets hold RDS and ElastiCache with no route to the internet at all. Route tables follow the tiers — IGW route for public, NAT for app-tier egress, none for data. Security groups are the primary control, referencing other SGs instead of CIDRs, with NACLs as a coarse second layer. I enable VPC Flow Logs on day one; the first time you debug a connectivity issue or face an audit, they pay for themselves.

Key points to hit

  • CIDR planning with growth headroom
  • 3-AZ spread, public/app/data tier separation
  • NAT Gateway for egress only
  • SG-references-SG over CIDR rules
  • Flow Logs from day one

Likely follow-ups

  • Why three AZs and not two?
    Two AZs survive one failure but quorum-based systems and capacity math work better with three; the marginal cost is small relative to the resilience.
  • Where do VPC endpoints fit?
    Interface/gateway endpoints for S3, ECR, DynamoDB keep traffic off NAT — both a security and a cost win.
  • How do you size subnets for EKS?
    Generously — the VPC CNI gives every pod a real IP, so I budget pod density per node, or enable prefix delegation.
AWS·02 When would you use VPC Peering vs Transit Gateway vs PrivateLink? Intermediate

Why they ask: Tests architectural judgment around connectivity patterns at scale.

Masterstroke answer

Peering is point-to-point and non-transitive with no bandwidth bottleneck — right for two or three VPCs that need a full mesh and nothing more. Transit Gateway is the hub-and-spoke answer once you're past roughly ten VPCs or you need on-prem connectivity via Direct Connect or VPN; it's transitive and centrally managed, but you pay a per-GB processing charge, so I keep chatty east-west traffic off it where I can. PrivateLink is different in kind: it exposes a single service endpoint across accounts without opening any network routing — ideal for internal SaaS-style services or vendor integrations where I explicitly don't want broad connectivity. At Takeda I used PrivateLink for exactly that reason: service-level exposure with zero blast radius into the network.

Key points to hit

  • Peering = simple, non-transitive
  • TGW = hub-and-spoke, transitive, per-GB cost
  • PrivateLink = service-level, no network access
  • Choose by scale and trust boundary

Likely follow-ups

  • Can you peer through a TGW to avoid peering limits?
    Yes — TGW makes connectivity transitive, which is the point; but model the data processing cost first.
  • PrivateLink bandwidth limits?
    It rides on NLB, so it scales well, but each endpoint is AZ-scoped — deploy endpoints in every AZ you consume from.
AWS·03 How does cross-region disaster recovery architecture work on AWS? Expert

Why they ask: Tests understanding of RPO/RTO tradeoffs and multi-region patterns.

Masterstroke answer

I never start with the pattern — I start with RPO and RTO targets, because they set the budget. Backup-and-restore is the floor: S3 cross-region replication, snapshot copies, AMIs — RPO and RTO in hours, nearly free. Pilot light pre-provisions core infrastructure scaled to zero with live database replication — RPO in minutes, RTO in tens of minutes. Warm standby runs a scaled-down full copy with Route 53 health-check failover — RPO seconds, RTO minutes. Multi-active uses DynamoDB Global Tables or Aurora Global Database with latency-based routing — near-zero both ways, but you pay for two full regions and you inherit the hard problems of write conflicts and data residency. Most workloads I've assessed honestly need pilot light; the business often asks for multi-active until they see the invoice.

Key points to hit

  • Lead with RPO/RTO, not patterns
  • Four patterns on a cost gradient
  • Route 53 health checks for failover
  • Aurora Global / DynamoDB Global Tables for active-active
  • Test the failover, not just the architecture

Likely follow-ups

  • How do you test DR?
    Scheduled game days with real failover to the DR region — an untested DR plan is a diagram, not a capability.
  • What about DNS TTLs?
    Keep them low (60s) on failover records, and remember some clients ignore TTLs — design retry behavior accordingly.
AWS·04 What's your approach to managing multiple AWS accounts? Intermediate

Why they ask: Tests organizational maturity and understanding of blast-radius isolation.

Masterstroke answer

AWS Organizations with a deliberate OU structure: a management account that does billing and org policy only, a security account holding GuardDuty delegated admin, Security Hub, and the org CloudTrail, shared-services for CI/CD and artifacts, a networking account owning Transit Gateway and Direct Connect, then workload OUs — dev, staging, prod — with an account per workload or team. SCPs at the OU level give me guardrails that no one can opt out of: deny unused regions, prevent disabling CloudTrail, require encryption. Control Tower automates account vending with those baselines pre-applied, and IAM Identity Center centralizes human access. The principle underneath all of it is blast radius: an account is the only hard isolation boundary AWS gives you, so I use accounts generously.

Key points to hit

  • OU hierarchy with purpose-built accounts
  • SCPs as non-negotiable guardrails
  • Control Tower for account vending
  • IAM Identity Center for SSO
  • Accounts as the real isolation boundary

Likely follow-ups

  • How granular should accounts be?
    Per workload per environment is my default; per team works for smaller orgs — the test is whether a compromise in one account can hurt another.
  • How do workload accounts reach shared services?
    TGW or PrivateLink from the networking/shared-services accounts, with Resource Access Manager for sharing subnets where it makes sense.
AWS·05 How do you decide between ECS Fargate, ECS on EC2, and EKS? Intermediate

Why they ask: Tests ability to make pragmatic compute decisions, not just default to Kubernetes.

Masterstroke answer

Fargate when I want zero infrastructure management and the workloads are stateless with predictable resource shapes — no DaemonSets, no host access, and that's fine. ECS on EC2 when I need GPUs, specific instance types, or want to sweat the cost with Savings Plans and the team can own the cluster layer. EKS when the Kubernetes ecosystem is the point — Helm, operators, CRDs, portability, or a team that already has deep K8s muscle. I'll say the unfashionable thing: if you don't have a concrete reason to run Kubernetes, ECS is simpler and cheaper to operate. I run EKS at Takeda because we needed the operator ecosystem and multi-team platform patterns — that's a reason; 'everyone uses K8s' is not.

Key points to hit

  • Fargate = serverless simplicity
  • ECS/EC2 = cost control + host access
  • EKS = ecosystem and platform patterns
  • Don't over-engineer — justify Kubernetes

Likely follow-ups

  • Fargate on EKS — when?
    For burst or isolation-sensitive namespaces; but per-vCPU cost is higher, so not for steady baseline load.
  • What did EKS cost you operationally?
    Upgrades, add-on compatibility, and IP management — real overhead that needs a platform team to absorb.
AWS·06 Describe Lambda cold start optimization strategies. Intermediate

Why they ask: Tests whether you've actually run Lambda in production, not just demos.

Masterstroke answer

First I measure whether cold starts actually matter — P99 latency on real traffic — because optimizing a non-problem is how you burn a sprint. If they matter: shrink the package (trim deps, layers for shared libs), pick a fast runtime (Python and Node start far quicker than JVM), lazy-load SDK clients outside the handler, and move to ARM64 — Graviton is both faster to start and cheaper. SnapStart takes Java cold starts from seconds to under one by snapshotting the initialized state. Provisioned Concurrency is the deliberate, paid answer for genuinely latency-critical paths. The scheduled-ping 'warmer' trick exists, but it's fragile and only keeps one instance warm — I treat it as a smell, not a solution. In my social-media pipeline on Lambda + EventBridge, cold starts were irrelevant; knowing when they don't matter is part of the answer.

Key points to hit

  • Measure P99 before optimizing
  • Package size, runtime choice, lazy init
  • ARM64/Graviton wins twice
  • SnapStart for Java
  • Provisioned Concurrency as the deliberate option

Likely follow-ups

  • Provisioned Concurrency vs reserved?
    Reserved caps concurrency for protection; provisioned pre-warms instances for latency — different problems.
  • VPC-attached Lambda cold starts?
    Largely solved since Hyperplane ENIs, but the first invocation in a new subnet config still pays a setup cost.
AWS·07 How would you architect a serverless event-driven application? Intermediate

Why they ask: Tests understanding of decoupled, asynchronous patterns.

Masterstroke answer

I separate ingress, routing, buffering, and compute. API Gateway handles synchronous ingress; EventBridge is the routing fabric — rules and event buses keep producers and consumers decoupled; SQS buffers anything bursty or retry-prone; SNS fans out. Lambda functions each do one thing. Step Functions orchestrates multi-step workflows — far better than chaining Lambdas because retries, error handling, and execution visibility come for free. Dead-letter queues sit on every queue and every async Lambda invocation; events that can't be processed must land somewhere inspectable, not vanish. EventBridge Pipes covers point-to-point flows without glue code. The discipline I enforce: every component independently deployable and independently scalable, and every event schema versioned, because the contract is the architecture in an event-driven system.

Key points to hit

  • EventBridge for routing, SQS for buffering
  • Step Functions over Lambda chaining
  • DLQs everywhere
  • Versioned event schemas
  • Independent deploy and scale per component

Likely follow-ups

  • Exactly-once processing?
    You don't get it — design idempotent consumers and use SQS FIFO with dedup IDs where ordering matters.
  • How do you trace a request across all this?
    Correlation IDs propagated in event payloads plus X-Ray or OpenTelemetry — observability is non-optional here.
AWS·08 How do you implement zero-trust networking in AWS? Expert

Why they ask: Tests modern security architecture thinking.

Masterstroke answer

Zero trust means the network location proves nothing — identity proves everything. Practically: IAM authenticates and authorizes every API call, so 'it's in the private subnet' is never a security argument. VPC endpoints keep AWS-service traffic off the public internet. Security groups become identity-aware by referencing other SGs rather than CIDR blocks. Service-to-service auth runs on IAM roles — IRSA for EKS pods so each workload has exactly its own permissions, no shared node role. Secrets rotate via Secrets Manager. mTLS or AWS Verified Access covers the application layer where needed. GuardDuty and Security Hub watch continuously. Network segmentation stays — but demoted to defense-in-depth, not the boundary. The mental shift I push teams through is from 'protect the perimeter' to 'verify every request,' and pharma compliance contexts like mine make that shift easier to sell, not harder.

Key points to hit

  • IAM as the primary control plane
  • VPC endpoints, SG-to-SG references
  • IRSA — per-pod identity
  • Verified Access / mTLS at app layer
  • Perimeter ≠ security; it's just one layer

Likely follow-ups

  • Where do teams struggle most?
    Legacy services with shared credentials — I inventory those first and put rotation plus scoped roles around them.
  • Does zero trust mean no private subnets?
    No — keep them; just stop treating placement as authorization.
AWS·09 How do you handle secrets management across environments? Intermediate

Why they ask: Tests practical operational security knowledge.

Masterstroke answer

Secrets Manager for anything that rotates — DB credentials, API keys — with Lambda rotation functions where supported. Parameter Store SecureString for slower-moving config; cheaper and simpler. For Kubernetes, External Secrets Operator syncs from Secrets Manager into K8s Secrets so the cluster never holds a second source of truth. CI/CD authenticates with OIDC federation — GitHub Actions assumes an IAM role scoped to repo and branch, and there are zero long-lived keys stored anywhere. Terraform reads secrets through data sources at plan time and marks variables sensitive; they never live in tfvars or get hardcoded. KMS customer-managed keys encrypt everything, with key policies controlling who can decrypt. The rule I hold the line on: a secret in a Git history is compromised, full stop — rotate it, don't debate it.

Key points to hit

  • Secrets Manager for rotation, Parameter Store for config
  • External Secrets Operator on EKS
  • OIDC federation in CI/CD — no static keys
  • sensitive=true and data sources in Terraform
  • Git exposure = immediate rotation

Likely follow-ups

  • How do you find leaked secrets?
    Pre-commit hooks plus continuous scanning (gitleaks/trufflehog) in CI and across history.
  • Secrets in container images?
    Never — BuildKit secret mounts at build time, runtime injection at deploy time.
AWS·10 When do you choose S3 vs EBS vs EFS vs FSx? Foundation

Why they ask: Tests understanding of storage tradeoffs — a common real-world decision.

Masterstroke answer

S3 for anything accessed by key over HTTP: backups, data lakes, static assets, model artifacts — unlimited scale, eleven nines durability, and the cheapest per GB. EBS is block storage for a single instance: databases, boot volumes, anything needing a filesystem with IOPS guarantees; multi-attach exists but I treat it as niche. EFS is managed NFS — multiple instances or pods sharing one filesystem, auto-scaling, but higher latency and cost than EBS, so I use it only when shared access is the actual requirement: shared config, CMS content, ML training data feeding many workers. FSx for Lustre when an HPC or ML workload needs serious throughput; FSx for NetApp ONTAP when lifting enterprise NAS workloads. The most common mistake I correct is EFS chosen for convenience where S3 plus a small redesign would be cheaper and more durable.

Key points to hit

  • S3 = objects/unlimited/cheapest
  • EBS = block, single-attach, IOPS
  • EFS = shared NFS, real shared-access need only
  • FSx = specialized HPC/NAS
  • Question 'shared filesystem' requirements

Likely follow-ups

  • EBS volume types?
    gp3 as default with independent IOPS/throughput tuning; io2 only for genuine high-IOPS databases.
  • S3 storage classes strategy?
    Lifecycle policies to Intelligent-Tiering or Glacier tiers — set them at bucket creation, not after the bill arrives.
AWS·11 Walk me through your approach to AWS cost optimization. Intermediate

Why they ask: Tests whether you treat cost as a first-class architectural concern.

Masterstroke answer

Visibility first: Cost and Usage Reports into S3, queried with Athena, and tagging enforced by policy — team, environment, project — because you can't optimize what you can't allocate. Then a strict order of operations: right-size from CloudWatch utilization data before any commitment, then purchasing — Compute Savings Plans for steady state, Spot for fault-tolerant workloads — then architectural moves: VPC endpoints to kill NAT processing charges, CloudFront in front of S3 for egress, Graviton for ~20% price-performance. Budgets with alerts, and a monthly review cadence so it stays a habit, not a project. At Takeda I drove a 25% AWS cost reduction this way, and the order mattered: the most common failure I see is teams buying Reserved Instances against oversized infrastructure — committing for years to waste.

Key points to hit

  • Tagging + CUR/Athena for visibility
  • Right-size BEFORE committing
  • Savings Plans over RIs for flexibility
  • NAT/egress architectural wins
  • Monthly cadence, owned by someone

Likely follow-ups

  • How did you get to 25%?
    Roughly: right-sizing and schedule-based shutdown of non-prod, Graviton migration, VPC endpoints for ECR/S3, and Savings Plans on the cleaned-up baseline.
  • Spot for production?
    Yes for stateless, interruption-tolerant workloads — with capacity-optimized allocation and graceful drain handling.
AWS·12 Your production database is at 95% CPU during business hours. What do you do? Expert

Why they ask: Scenario — tests diagnosis discipline versus reflexive scaling.

Masterstroke answer

I don't scale first; I diagnose. Performance Insights to see top SQL and wait events — is this one bad query or genuine aggregate load? A handful of queries: fix them — missing indexes, full scans, N+1 patterns from the ORM; that's usually an order-of-magnitude win for free. Read-heavy load: add read replicas and route reads there, plus ElastiCache for the hot set. Connection churn: RDS Proxy for pooling. Only after optimization, if it's genuinely at capacity, do I scale vertically — and at that same moment I start the architectural plan (caching layer, CQRS, or sharding) because the ceiling will return. Scaling up without a root cause is renting time, not solving the problem, and I say that to stakeholders in exactly those words.

Key points to hit

  • Performance Insights before action
  • Query optimization first — biggest free win
  • Read replicas + cache for read load
  • RDS Proxy for connection storms
  • Vertical scale buys time; plan the real fix simultaneously

Likely follow-ups

  • What if it's a lock contention issue?
    Wait events show it — then it's transaction design and isolation levels, not capacity at all.
  • How do you prevent recurrence?
    Query performance budgets in CI and alerting on plan regressions, not just CPU.

AI·01 Design a production RAG pipeline on AWS. Walk me through the architecture and key decisions. Expert

Why they ask: The defining GenAI architecture question — tests end-to-end thinking beyond a demo notebook.

Masterstroke answer

Four stages: ingestion, indexing, retrieval, generation. Ingestion: documents land in S3, an event-driven pipeline (EventBridge → Lambda or Step Functions) extracts, cleans, and chunks them. Chunking is the most underrated decision — I start around 500–1,000 tokens with overlap, but structure-aware chunking that respects headings and tables beats fixed-size almost every time. Indexing: an embedding model (Bedrock Titan or Cohere) writes vectors into OpenSearch Serverless or pgvector on Aurora — I pick by what the team can operate, not benchmark charts. Retrieval: hybrid search — vector similarity plus BM25 keyword — with a reranker on top; pure vector search quietly misses exact terms like part numbers and drug names. Generation: the LLM gets retrieved context with strict prompt structure and citation requirements. I built exactly this locally with LangChain, ChromaDB, and Ollama before mapping it to managed services — and the offline evaluation harness (retrieval hit-rate, groundedness) mattered more than any single component choice.

Key points to hit

  • Chunking strategy drives quality
  • Hybrid retrieval (vector + keyword) + rerank
  • Pick vector store by operability
  • Citations and grounding in the prompt contract
  • Evaluation harness from day one

Likely follow-ups

  • How do you keep the index fresh?
    Event-driven re-indexing on document change, with chunk-level upserts and tombstones for deletions.
  • When is RAG the wrong tool?
    When the knowledge is stable and compact — fine-tuning or even a long system prompt can be simpler and cheaper.
  • How do you measure RAG quality?
    Retrieval metrics (recall@k) separately from generation metrics (faithfulness, answer relevance) — conflating them hides which stage failed.
AI·02 Explain the Model Context Protocol (MCP) and when you'd build an MCP server versus a traditional API integration. Expert

Why they ask: Tests current agentic-AI architecture knowledge — increasingly a differentiator question.

Masterstroke answer

MCP standardizes how LLM applications discover and invoke external capabilities — tools, resources, and prompts — over a defined protocol, instead of every team hand-wiring bespoke function-calling glue. The server exposes typed tools with schemas; any MCP-compatible client can discover and call them. I'd build an MCP server when the capability should be reusable across multiple AI clients and when I want the tool contract — including auth and read/write scope — defined once, centrally. I deployed one in production on AWS for a Patient Connect Ops use case at Takeda: read-only log-tailing tools over SSH to EC2, deliberately scoped so the agent could observe but never mutate. A traditional API integration still wins when there's exactly one consumer and no agentic client in the loop — MCP earns its keep at the point of reuse and standardization, like any protocol.

Key points to hit

  • Standardized tool/resource discovery for LLM clients
  • Typed tool contracts defined once
  • Reuse across agents is the trigger to adopt
  • Scope tools deliberately — read-only by default
  • Real production example beats theory

Likely follow-ups

  • How did you secure your MCP server?
    Read-only tool design, least-privilege IAM and SSH credentials, network isolation, and audit logging of every tool invocation.
  • MCP vs OpenAPI function calling?
    Function calling is per-model glue; MCP is a client-agnostic protocol with lifecycle, discovery, and resource semantics.
AI·03 How do you architect an AI agent system that's safe to run against production infrastructure? Expert

Why they ask: Tests whether you've thought about blast radius for autonomous systems — rare and valuable.

Masterstroke answer

The same way I'd onboard a new junior engineer with superhuman speed and zero fear: scoped credentials, supervised actions, and an audit trail. Concretely: agents get their own IAM identities with least privilege — read-only as the default posture, and write capabilities individually justified and gated. Destructive or stateful actions go behind human-in-the-loop approval; the agent proposes, a person disposes. Every tool invocation is logged with inputs and outputs for audit and replay. I sandbox execution — separate accounts or projects so a confused agent can't reach production state. Rate limits and budget caps bound the damage of loops. And I design the tool surface itself defensively: my production MCP deployment exposed log-tailing but structurally could not restart a service, because the safest permission is the one that doesn't exist.

Key points to hit

  • Agent = identity with least privilege
  • Read-only default; writes individually gated
  • Human-in-the-loop for destructive actions
  • Full audit log of tool calls
  • Capability absence beats permission checks

Likely follow-ups

  • Prompt injection against agents?
    Treat all retrieved/external content as untrusted input — never let it elevate tool scope; validate tool arguments server-side.
  • How do you test agent behavior?
    Scenario suites with adversarial inputs in a sandbox, plus replay of logged production traces.
AI·04 Compare Amazon Bedrock against self-hosting open models on EKS. How do you decide? Intermediate

Why they ask: Tests pragmatic build-vs-buy judgment applied to GenAI infrastructure.

Masterstroke answer

Bedrock gives me managed inference, model choice across providers, no GPU capacity management, per-token pricing, and the compliance posture — data not used for training, PrivateLink access, regional control — that enterprise and pharma contexts demand. Self-hosting on EKS (vLLM or TGI on GPU node groups) wins when there's sustained high-volume inference where per-token economics flip, hard data-residency constraints, a fine-tuned model Bedrock can't serve, or latency requirements needing full control. The honest math: self-hosting means GPU capacity planning, model updates, inference optimization, and on-call — a platform team's worth of work. My default is Bedrock until the token bill or a hard constraint argues otherwise, and I treat it like any commodity-vs-differentiator call: serving infrastructure is rarely where an enterprise's competitive advantage lives.

Key points to hit

  • Bedrock = managed, compliant, per-token
  • Self-host = control, residency, volume economics
  • GPU ops burden is a real cost line
  • Commodity vs differentiator framing
  • Default managed; justify the exception

Likely follow-ups

  • Where's the volume crossover?
    It varies by model and utilization — model it explicitly; a 70B on underutilized GPUs is the worst of both worlds.
  • Fine-tuning options on Bedrock?
    Customization exists for some models; if your tuning needs exceed it, that's a legitimate self-host trigger.
AI·05 What does LLMOps mean in practice, and how does it differ from traditional MLOps? Intermediate

Why they ask: Tests whether you can map operational discipline onto GenAI — the core of an AI platform role.

Masterstroke answer

Traditional MLOps centers on the training loop: feature pipelines, experiment tracking, model registries, retraining triggers. LLMOps mostly operates models you didn't train, so the center of gravity shifts to everything around inference: prompt management with versioning and rollback — prompts are deployable artifacts and deserve the same rigor as code; evaluation pipelines, because there's no single accuracy number — you build eval suites with LLM-as-judge plus human review and run them like regression tests on every prompt or model change; cost and latency observability per request, since tokens are the new compute bill; guardrails for input and output — PII, injection, topic boundaries; and version pinning of models, because providers update behavior under stable names. Coming from DevOps, I map it directly: prompts are code, evals are tests, guardrails are policy-as-code, token spend is FinOps. The disciplines transfer; the artifacts changed.

Key points to hit

  • Prompts as versioned, deployable artifacts
  • Eval suites as regression tests
  • Token-level cost/latency observability
  • Guardrails on input and output
  • Model version pinning

Likely follow-ups

  • What breaks first without LLMOps?
    Silent quality drift — a provider model update or an 'innocent' prompt tweak degrades output and nobody notices until users do.
  • Tooling you'd reach for?
    Langfuse or Phoenix for tracing/evals, Bedrock Guardrails or custom filters, and plain CI for prompt regression suites.
AI·06 How do you control and optimize LLM inference costs at scale? Intermediate

Why they ask: GenAI bills surprise organizations the way data transfer once did — tests FinOps maturity for AI.

Masterstroke answer

Same discipline as cloud FinOps: visibility, then optimization, then commitment. Visibility: per-request token logging tagged by feature, team, and tenant — you can't allocate a token bill you don't itemize. Optimization, in order of leverage: route by task complexity — a classifier or extraction task doesn't need a frontier model, and model routing (Haiku-class for simple, Opus-class for hard) routinely cuts 60–80% of spend; prompt caching for repeated system-prompt prefixes; trim context aggressively — RAG retrieving ten chunks when three suffice is paying for noise; batch APIs for anything async, typically half price; cap max output tokens. Commitment: provisioned throughput once baseline volume is proven. The cultural piece matters too — engineers see latency but not cost, so I put token spend on the same dashboards as error rates.

Key points to hit

  • Per-request token attribution
  • Model routing by task complexity — biggest lever
  • Prompt caching and context discipline
  • Batch APIs for async work
  • Cost on the same dashboard as latency

Likely follow-ups

  • How do you route without hurting quality?
    Eval suites per task tier — route down until the eval says stop, with fallback escalation on low confidence.
  • Who owns the token budget?
    Same as cloud cost: the feature team owns it, the platform team makes it visible and provides cheaper paths.
AI·07 How would you design guardrails for an enterprise LLM application in a regulated industry? Expert

Why they ask: Pharma/finance interviewers ask this constantly — tests compliance-aware AI architecture.

Masterstroke answer

Defense in depth across four layers. Input: PII detection and redaction before anything reaches the model, prompt-injection screening, and topic filters for clearly out-of-scope requests. K8S·01 Explain the Kubernetes control plane components and what each does. Foundation

K8S·03 How do you plan capacity for an EKS cluster? Intermediate

K8S·07 Explain the VPC CNI plugin in EKS and its implications. Expert

Why they ask: EKS-specific — tests whether you understand the networking tradeoffs.

Masterstroke answer

The VPC CNI gives every pod a real VPC IP from the subnet — ENI secondary IPs — so pods are first-class VPC citizens: security groups for pods, NACLs, Flow Logs, and Direct Connect all just work with pod traffic, no overlay translation. The cost is IP consumption, twice over: instance types cap pods per node by ENI and IP limits — a t3.medium tops out around 17 pods, an m5.large around 29 — and the subnets themselves drain fast in a busy cluster. Cilium in overlay mode is the escape hatch if you truly need density over native integration — but you give up the thing that made the VPC CNI valuable.

Key points to hit

  • Real VPC IPs — native SG/Flow Log integration
  • Per-instance pod limits via ENI math
  • Subnet exhaustion is the silent failure
  • Prefix delegation first, secondary CIDR second
  • Overlay CNI trades away the native benefits

Likely follow-ups

  • How do you spot IP exhaustion early?
    Alert on subnet free-IP counts and pods stuck in ContainerCreating with ENI attach errors.
  • Security groups for pods — when?
    When specific workloads need tighter network policy than the node SG — e.g., only the payments pods may reach the payments DB.

TF·07 What is GitOps and how does it differ from traditional CI/CD? Intermediate

Why they ask: Tests understanding of modern deployment philosophy.

Masterstroke answer

GitOps makes Git the single source of truth and inverts the deployment direction: instead of CI pushing changes into the cluster with kubectl credentials, an in-cluster agent — ArgoCD or Flux — pulls from Git and continuously reconciles actual state to declared state. Four things fall out of that inversion. Audit: the commit history is the deployment history — who, what, when, why. Rollback: a git revert, no special tooling. Drift correction: the agent notices when cluster state diverges from Git and fixes it automatically — manual kubectl edits literally don't survive. Security: the CI system no longer holds cluster credentials at all; it only writes to Git, which collapses the attack surface dramatically. The operational shift that surprises teams: the cluster becomes disposable in principle — Git plus the agent can rebuild it — which changes how you think about DR and environment provisioning. ArgoCD runs our Takeda platform delivery, and the UI alone cut incident-time deployment questions to 'look at the screen.'

Key points to hit

  • Pull-based reconciliation vs push
  • Git history = audit trail
  • Rollback = git revert
  • Drift auto-corrected
  • CI never touches the cluster

Likely follow-ups

  • ArgoCD vs Flux?
    ArgoCD's UI and ecosystem win for multi-team enterprises; Flux is leaner and CLI-native — I default ArgoCD for orgs, Flux for small platform-savvy teams.
  • Secrets in GitOps?
    External Secrets referencing AWS, or SOPS-encrypted in repo — declared in Git, materialized in cluster, plaintext nowhere.
TF·08 What's your approach to testing infrastructure code? Expert

Why they ask: Tests maturity — most teams skip IaC testing.

Masterstroke answer

A pyramid, same as application code. Base: static analysis — fmt, validate, tflint, Checkov — on every commit; fast, catches syntax, deprecations, and known misconfigurations. Middle: plan validation — terraform plan as JSON, asserted programmatically with OPA: expected resources present, and critically, no unexpected destroys; an assertion that the plan never deletes a database has paid for itself everywhere I've installed it. Top: integration tests with Terratest — actually apply into an ephemeral account, verify behavior (endpoint reachable, encryption on, ports right), destroy. Expensive, so they run on module releases, not every commit. And one layer no tool replaces: a human reading the plan. 'Why is this recreating the RDS instance?' is a question only a person asks, which is exactly why my pipelines post plans into PRs — the review is the test the automation can't write.

Key points to hit

  • Static (fast) → plan assertions (medium) → Terratest (slow)
  • Assert no-unexpected-destroys
  • Ephemeral accounts for integration tests
  • Human plan review is a test layer
  • Cost-tier the test frequency

Likely follow-ups

  • How do you test modules across provider versions?
    Matrix builds in CI against the pinned and next provider versions — upgrade surprises caught in the module repo, not in prod.
  • Is Terratest worth the Go investment?
    For modules where a regression destroys data or networks, yes; for a tagging module, plan assertions suffice — match rigor to blast radius.
TF·09 Your Terraform apply is stuck and you can't release the state lock. What do you do? Expert

Why they ask: Scenario — tests calm operational judgment under a classic failure.

Masterstroke answer

First, verify it's actually stuck and not a legitimate run — check CI history and ask the team, because force-unlocking under a live apply is how you corrupt state for real. If it's a crashed process: take the Lock ID from the error and terraform force-unlock it. Then, before any other operation, terraform plan to assess state consistency — a crash mid-apply can leave partially recorded resources, and the plan output tells me whether state and reality still agree. If state is damaged: S3 versioning, restore the last good object — this is the moment that bucket versioning policy justifies itself. Worst case, state pull, inspect, and surgically repair or re-import. Prevention is the real answer: pipeline timeouts so runs can't hang forever, -lock-timeout so queued runs wait politely instead of failing, and alerts on runs exceeding expected duration. I've run this exact playbook; the discipline is the pause before force-unlock, not the command itself.

Key points to hit

  • Confirm crashed vs live before anything
  • force-unlock with the Lock ID
  • Plan immediately to assess consistency
  • S3 versioning restore for corruption
  • Timeouts + lock-timeout as prevention

Likely follow-ups

  • Partially created resources after the crash?
    Plan shows the divergence — import what exists but isn't in state, or remove state entries for what doesn't exist.
  • Two CI runs racing?
    That's what locking prevented — fix the pipeline concurrency settings so per-stack runs serialize.
TF·10 How do you migrate a team from manually-managed infrastructure to Terraform? Expert
